Interview for WEH by Alex Franquelli A question that may sound almost banal coming from a non-Norwegian speaker, but that may explain your approach to music to an audience that may get to know you for the first time with ‘En Natt Kom Doed’. Why the choice of recording most of the tracks in your native language rather than English?

-This is just a matter of trying something slightly different than what I am used to. The first time I tried out lyrics in Norwegian, aside from the “Likbør” cover, was in the “Origins” recording in 2010. I felt that it worked out very good, and that this was something I would get more involved in. And there came a time when it felt more natural to do the words in my own tongue, even though the international appeal may suffer from this, but I don’t know. One is tempted to define your music as ‘neofolk’, probably in lack of a better label. Does it bother you or would you agree with me that it is folk we are talking about here: one that goes back to the roots of a genre written and played with a guitar, some faint keyboards and a hint of percussions. It is purely folk. Played by a contemporary but folk nonetheless.

-I would agree that this is music that very well can be placed under the “Folk” label. I didn’t have any consideration for genre when I started up with Weh, but it is what it is, and somewhere along the way some people have called it simply “Dark Folk” and I think that sounds quite good. Your music has followed a natural evolution from the demos which have caused quite a stir in Scandinavia and not only there. Where do you think your music can be taken? Do you have any addition in mind in the foreseeable future?

-The evolution you mention may not be monumental, but the songwriting gets a little bit stronger for each recording. And I can’t really predict where Weh is headed in the future, because I tend to let the songs and the recording process take their own direction and let coincidences play a part. My guess is that there will not be any major changes to anything, but rather some additions of other instruments and elements of composing, whatever I feel works good at the time. The foundation will nonetheless be acoustic guitar based music with focus on the darker emotions. Nature as well as human interactions seem to be at the core of your music, which appears to be one thing with the landscape it seems to describe. How strong is your connection with the Norwegian environment? How much does it influence you?

-The Norwegian nature has always had a big influence on my music and lyrics. My connection with it is simply that I love it and like to spend time in it. By that I don’t mean running up and down every mountain top I can find with map and compass, but rather taking a walk in the woods some time and maybe just sit down by a river or lake, taking it all in. We are just specks of dust compared to Mother Nature and the mighty elements, and I am highly aware of that. On the other hand, what strikes me is your ability to balance your musical and cultural roots with a deeply Western songwriting. Crooners and songwriters such as Leonard Cohen, Tom Waits, and the French and Italian schools come to mind. Is there anyone in particular that inspiring you these days and who has had an influence on your development as a musician?

-It would be an impossible task to name just a few of the musical sources of inspiration, but I can say that my taste includes all kinds of artists – from Darkthrone to John Denver, Grieg to trance music, Iron Maiden to Johnny Cash, you get the idea. Acoustic music is sometimes seen as a secondary means of expression for musicians – especially in the underground. It seldom appears as a primary occupation and it is almost always seen as a spin-off of a main project. In the future, will you try to take the opposite path and start a band as an alternative to your career as an acoustic songwriter?

-This is an interesting question, and Weh is absolutely a primary occupation for me. I have many times played with the idea of doing a more metal related project, but I have not done anything out of it yet. Maybe I’ll do it or maybe I won’t. The main focus will still be Weh, as long as I keep making and recording music. ‘One Night Death Came’ (I hope I got this one right). What do you refer to? Is there a specific event that has inspired you?

-Yes, you got the translation right. This title doesn’t refer to any particular event, but rather it refers to my fascination and respect of death as a subject. It can also refer to each and every song on the album, be it “Oedegaarden” which deals with The Black Plague, “A Testament Of Time” which is about the passing of time, or “The Unborn” which describes some kind of armageddon. Although your music is as far from black metal as it is from pop, I guess Norwegian musicians will have to come to terms with the fact that, at least for the next 30 years, they will automatically be related to the black metal scene. Have you been part of it and has it been important in your growth?

-My part in the Black Metal scene is that I for the greater part of my life has been a fan of the music, in the same way I am a fan of heavy metal, death metal, thrash metal... As well as a fan of completely different music. There was something in the mood of the early nineties Black Metal that appealed in the first place, and some say I have a similar mood in my music. There’s no denying that the Norwegian Black Metal scene was a successful one, but I think the overall interest has normalised a bit in the last decade or more. I am reasonably sure that lots of people will want to see you live after listening to ‘En Natt Kom Doed’. Do you usually play solo and is there any plan for a tour? -And as flattering as that may be, I am reasonably sure that I will never perform live. I consider myself to be a recording artist, end of story. But I can’t rule out the possibility altogether, of course. In the recent past I have come across a number of acts and bands from Norway and I know quite a few musicians who are moving to Oslo because they regard that music scene as ‘booming’. Is that true? Can you feel that something is really happening in there? -I don’t have a connection to any music scene at all really, so whatever happens in this environment is passing me by, I’m afraid. It is of course positive that Norway is generating more interest from the outside world, and it is probably deserved. And if this means that the interest in Weh is growing too, I can only be grateful.
