Interview for BLOOD AXIS by Alex Franquelli PLEASE, NOTE THAT OUR FINAL DEADLINE IS:

21th OF MAY

There is a vague sense of disorientation in ‘Born Again’, almost a dangerous flirting with loneliness that is very well expressed by the simple, raw strokes of the guitar, the echo of violins in the background and the disillusioned approach to spoken vocals. I like to think that this is due to the troubled nature of the album, which has seen the light through years of wandering in at least two continents. By all means, I am not implying that the album is “out of focus” but the sadness that is present here was maybe the latent rage that marked, for example, “Absinthe: La Folie Verte”. Was the long journey itself that triggered this state of mind or was it maybe the opposite?

You are a perceptive listener: there is absolutely a sense of disillusionment that permeates the album. It is a natural development that comes with age. Ironically the longer you are alive, the more you realize how fragile and transient life is ... things grow, change, become stagnant, fall apart, die, decompose, yet somehow new life breaks through it all again and again. This is true both on the material as well as the spiritual plane. So it is the grand scheme that governs us all, and an ongoing recipe for tragedy. If one can express tragedy and disillusionment with insight and beauty, then that is a worthy achievement.

In more than one occasion, while listening to ‘Born Again’, I had the impression that the late Johnny Cash is now a tangible influence of yours. Is it true or is it just that neo-folk has completed its evolution and has returned to its origins?

It never occurred to me that Johnny Cash might be an influence, but now that you have suggested it, I think you may well be right. Certainly we have listened to his music for years--nearly for our whole lifetimes--and have only the highest respect for the man. My parents had the San Quentin and Folsom Prison LPs in their record collection and I remember playing these over and over again a nine-year-old child. Robert and Annabel are both great admirers of the “Man in Black” too. But I don’t think this has anything to do with “neo-folk,” which is a term that I don’t really understand.

‘Born Again’ stands out as a piece of art that is so essential in its development and this makes the album sound so natural, almost instinctive. How much do you let sudden inspiration take charge of your composition rather than letting logic thoughts spoil your ideas and their purity?

Most all of our songs are composed spontaneously, either by one of us alone, or with two or three of us together. Usually there is a simple melodic idea, which then mutates the more we play it. A lyrical idea is usually there too and this combines with the music and together they take on a life of their own. Creation can’t be controlled, only directed. On this album you collaborated with several renowned musicians. How did you limit their contribution in order to obtain such supreme simplicity and dramatic force?

All the guest musicians are close friends of ours, so we understand each other to a certain degree. Whenever we felt that a particular song might benefit from outside collaboration, we presented it to the right person and they brought their own magic to it.

Among other instruments, you’ve used a bodhrán, some lyrics are in medieval English and some in Middle High German. Many other artists have seen their increased access to unusual instruments and resources take its tool over their music whilst, in your case, you still manage to keep the situation under control. How did you come up with such a diverse range of musical solutions?

Besides my voice, the bodhrán is my main instrument, so it was natural to use it. The texts first inspired me, both for what they say and for the power of the old languages. Since these languages are related to modern English and modern German, they give a strange feeling of something familiar and yet very distant at the same time. So it was better to keep them in the original. I made faithful translations of the texts for the booklet so that the words would not be obscure. As far as the guest musicians, they played the instruments which best fulfilled the purpose and mood of the song. This is just a natural result due to the circle of people we know and respect.

Annabel’s contribution has very much increased throughout time. Is the composition perfectly split between the two of you right now?

The binding force behind the music is me, but Blood Axis has always been a collaborative endeavor. Robert was there almost from the very beginning, and his spirit is always there even though he lives halfway across the continent apart from us. Annabel and I often play music together and this influences the recordings. She has a finely tuned sense of melody. Often she will come up with the basic musical line for a song, and then we will slowly modify this until it fits our voices and the words. Ultimately the compositions result from a musical dialogue between myself, Annabel, Robert, guest musicians, the song itself, and other spirits...

Can you tell us more about the time when you decided you had had enough of the music business and took the decision to work on Nietzsche’s ‘The Antichrist’? It seems that you haven’t completely abandoned that period (and for what we can hear now, that period hasn’t left you at all). Was it part of a maturation process? If so, how much has it changed you?

I am thoroughly ambivalent about the music business. I have never tried to make a living from it, and I think it’s better that way. Besides, I am also too interested in other things to dedicate myself entirely to music. Personally I find good books as beautiful and important as musical releases, so therefore we (Annabel and I) have also published some special printed editions, journals, and also significant translations from other languages. Any serious projects like this will contribute to a maturation and learning process, and anytime you set a goal or challenge for yourself and eventually accomplish it, this changes you.

What is your take on contemporary politics? How much have your views changed with time?

My views remain as skeptical and cynical as ever. I am in favor of smaller structures, greater independence and self-reliance, and freedom from thought-police of any kind. So efforts which increase these kinds of things are positive, and efforts which decrease them are negative. There are many subjects which, according to modern society, should not be touched and the sole act of messing with them automatically labels you a “dangerous and subversive individual”. Extreme politics is surely one of those. How tired are you of having to justify yourself for your past beliefs and current political curiosity?

I have always resisted the tendency to justify myself. As my friend Monte Cazazza once said: “Time will vindicate me.”

Any chance to see another Total War performance any time soon?

No, unless you watch the news. I happened to read ‘Lords Of Chaos’ before getting to know you as a musician and I found the book to be an excellent picture of what had gone and was partially still going on in the metal scene. What is left of that scene?

Our book was an attempt to document a very volatile period in the history of Black Metal. Now it is a job for others to tell the story of what happened after that...
