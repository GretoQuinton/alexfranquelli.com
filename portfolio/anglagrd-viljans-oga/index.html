Änglagård “Viljans Öga”

for All About Jazz

Complexity can sometimes be considered a form of art in itself. It seldom follows simplicity but it almost always depicts reality as it first appears before the subconscious elaborates it. Complexity is impressionism in its purest form: its faithful depiction of the present may be deemed naïve and unsophisticated, but it remains an unfiltered and pure representation of the being. Less than 20 years ago, Änglagård’s albums, “Hybris” and “Epilog”, caused a stir in the world of progressive. Their blending of the archetypes of progressive, Scandinavian folk and fusion was a synthesis of the aesthetics of the time: one which was vaguely influenced by Van Der Graaf Generator’s cerebral harmonies, King’s Crimson jazz impetus and the tradition of the Swedish polska.

Despite the long hiatus, the band is back with an album whose sound is a natural, thoughtful excursus on the state of progressive rock today. Not only is the final result a fresh take on an otherwise artistically comatose genre, but its taste for sonic beauty, its beautiful representation of the world (strictly in minor) and the odd time signatures, all make “Viljans Öga” unmistakably Änglagård. In both a good and a bad way. The complex patterns on “Sorgmantel” or “Langtans Klocka”, for instance, loosely follow the diktats of certain avant-garde (think James Dillon, Arvo Pärt and some John Adams) while the intricate melodies pay homage to fusion jazz by making solid references to the likes of Pat Metheny and Joe Zawinul. The elaborateness, the shifting rhythms, the elegant composition and the clever arrangements are therefore not an end to themselves: they are the medium through which the mellotron, the piano and the flute draw the sonic paintings of a perfect and ideal pastoral setting.

If the sound still appears to be modern, this is mainly due to the wise balance between tradition and experimentation, which is synthesized in a track like “Sorgmantel”, where flutist Anna Holmgren leads the rest of the bands into bold dissonances before letting the song rest on a quietly albeit sinister folk texture.

“Viljans Öga” is a complex album in an effortless way. This powerful contradiction lies at the very basis of Änglagård’s legacy in a way that shows the delicate relationship between musical experimentation and folk tradition in a post-industrial society. The idyllic scenery is tarnished by nostalgia (hence the unending minor key) in a way that is so typically contemporary and Scandinavian: it’s colorful, it’s pleasant, it’s challenging, it’s complex. Easy.
