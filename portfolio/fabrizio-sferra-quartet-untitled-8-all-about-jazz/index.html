Fabrizio Sferra Quartet “Untitled#28”

It sounds great. It really does. It happens every time a record flows in such a way that allows the listener to actually enjoy its variations, contrasts and colors. Yes, colors: Untitled#28 is so full of them. Musically, for instance, the sound shaped by drummer Fabrizio Sferra's quartet is one which toys with the listener's perception of intensity and timbres in a way that appears to be utterly spontaneous, rather than planned. Sferra is undoubtedly one of the biggest names in Italian jazz at the moment. His collaboration with trumpeter Enrico Rava on his 2003's aptly titled Full Of Life album contributed to make Sferra's name appear on many a radar, but it is fair to say that his work with pianist Danilo Rea and bassist Enzo Pietropaoli in Doctor 3 was already worth more than a mention a lustrum earlier. If the motive behind Doctor 3 was an elegantly ironic approach to mainstream music (be it pop, folk or jazz) with their rendition of classics such as Sting's "Wrapped around your fingers" and Neil Young's "Harvest," the key to understanding Untitled#28 is simplicity. The multi-textural dynamics maneuvered by Giovanni Guidi's excellent piano and Sferra's polyrhythmic drumming perfectly match Joe Rehmer's fine bass work on Dan Kinzelman's tenor saxophone. The final result is effective, graceful, refined and yet simple in its beauty. Mentioning the word 'color' at the beginning of the review was an almost necessary and unavoidable act , as the record betrays the leader's Mediterranean approach to tonality by adding a certain warmth to the already rich mixture of dilated and convulse tempos. And the extemporized improvisation on tracks like "The Snow Child" and "The Accomplices" is somehow rich in this chromatic value of which Sferra appears to be the man responsible. His drumming is at times almost detached from its mere rhythmic tasks while adding some percussive motifs to the vaporous textures laid by the brilliant intercourse taking place between the saxophone and the bass. The fact that no instrument seems to lead the extremely fluid discourse is testament of the fact that the real added value on this record is indeed the meticulous teamwork, even in a series of ever-changing (and challenging) patterns that is such a fine album. Composition is king here, and the fact that the final result if void of the self-indulgent intricacy (too) many quartets seem to aspire to, makes Untitled#28 an exhilarating and fascinating listen. The kind one would expect from a superb set of musicians balancing themselves between Keith Jarrett's chimeric standards and a pop-minded, latent aesthetics. The language spoken is essential and simple, but the argument is rich, complex, demanding and utterly colorful.
