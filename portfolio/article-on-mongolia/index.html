Article on Mongolia (no title yet)

(Alex Franquelli)

“The sky, the sky is bright!” The voice of the longhaired girl shakes our morning abruptly, as we open our eyes to the almost mystical vision ahead of us. The sun is a warm light reflected on the restless water of the river Onon and the steppe we glimpse beyond the aquatic path disappears in a blur of sensual lambent radiance. The joke was on us: we had slept well into the morning despite our promise of an early rise. No storm was in sight but the rounded woman who had cooked for us the night before gave us a word of advice while handing us generous servings of mutton and cabbage.

It must be Sunday, but the people bustling about in and outside Binder don’t seem to care too much. A youngster passes me by on his way to the river and I feel compelled to ask him what the fuss is all about. I strip my sentence of all useless adjectives and verbs while adding emphasis as I struggle to make him understand my question. “Why noise? Why people run?”. The guy, half rapper, half herder, looks at his fake Chinese Rolex he acquired somewhere, somehow and looks at me with a piercing smile. “Tin. They go to tin!” Mines! He’s right. The sky, the steppe, the animals and mines. Of coal, fluorite, iron ore, phosphates, uranium, gold, wolfram, molybdenum, copper and lead. And tin. Binder lives on it. Two roads, one shop and a formerly nomadic population of approximately 200 people devoted to accumulating natural resources in a place which less than a hundred years ago simply did not exist.

The rapid urbanisation and industrialisation growth processes promoted by the former Communist regime until 1992 and by western capitalism afterwards, is slowly eroding a millenary society. China, the big neighbour of the south, needs energy, so the soft coal burning in the power plants near the capital Ulaanbaatar pollute the environment while calling for manpower to gather in the squalid suburbs we encountered on our way here. As If urbanisation and pollution were not enough, zud, a well-known phenomenon that refers to the freezing of the soil for months, thus making grazing for animals impossible, is now practically becoming a yearly occurrence. The desert claiming a vast amount of land every year is turning parts of Mongolia into something else; a perfect hybrid where nomadic life disappears to give way to buildings erected in the middle of nowhere that you cannot refer to as either ‘cities’, or ‘villages’. We passed a few of these while travelling on the roadless landscape. If it was indeed possible to read and observe minds, the silence in the van could easily be interpreted as everyone wondering what life would be like living on the seventh floor of one of those concrete agglomerates right between the sand and the blue sky. Solid, probably warm, surely windy, “must be at work on time”. Somewhere, somehow.

In the meantime the rapper-herder has now re-appeared on the other side of the sacred river Onon. He waves at me right before crossing the waters again, this time on his Nissan tractor. I smile and raise my right hand while packing up my tent. Two out of every five Mongolians still make their living herding animals (mainly sheep, goats and camels), but last year, according to the World Bank, the nation’s economy was the fastest growing on the planet thanks to the mining boom.

There are contrasts everywhere and this becomes quite visible in Ulaanbaatar. The lack of a reliable transition is creating enormous rifts in a civil society whose shape is being moulded by its own impending, urgent needs, rather than by its own desires. “This city is what it is because our citizens are what they are”, said Plato while looking at Athens. But what is a city in Mongolia today? My thoughts are swept away by the noise of the engine my local, improbable rendition of Eminem stops right next to me. “Picture!”. I did not take one of him bravely doing what he does every day and I am genuinely sorry. He smiles and (hopefully) thanks me in Mongolian while leaving me in a cloud of black, thick smoke.

As our van starts to head south, towards the Gobi. The desert means business nowadays and the huge mine of Oyu Tolgoi, co-owned by the mining giants Ivanhoe Mines, Rio Tinto and the Mongolian government will soon become the world’s third largest gold and copper mine. There is no road linking these two regions (or any region at all, for all that matters) and the journey will include a substantial amount of bouncing around in our green van. I grab the big tank of water and place it right beside me on the van. “They need water down there. The rivers are drying and it hasn’t rained for months. Plus the big mine needs water to process the copper ore. Not fun”, our translator says while nonchalantly brushing her hair as if we were about to embark on a pleasure cruise on the sand.

The Onon river is now behind us as we leave Binder on our left. Once again, the landscape goes back to all shades of green and it will remain like this for miles before we enter the arid desertified territory of the south. Gers, the traditional Mongolian yurts of the nomads, become more sparse as we travel, while caravans of trucks start to appear on the horizon carrying their black load towards China. Tonight we will camp on the border of the Gobi. Tomorrow the lunar landscape will welcome us to a grey, sandy expanse. Our driver said he thinks he knows where Oyu Tolgoi is and that he just has to follow his instinct and the rare electricity poles. Just like Mongolia itself, we will eventually get where we have to get. Somewhere, somehow. Sometime.
