Article for Dartford Living

Written by Alex Franquelli

.

When writing an article the other month, I began to wonder what kind of silence is distinctive of London, and I found myself right in the middle of the streets of the City where for a few quiet moments everything went silent. Children stopped stepping in puddles, sirens faded under a big invisible shadow and the most faint noises became what they had originally been: pure silence. I am sure every city has got its moments of solemnity, even in the middle of the working week and I was lucky enough to catch a sonic glimpse of London’s mute colours. Silence, it must be said, is music of another world because, as one of last century’s greatest composers John Cage said: “total absence of sound simply does not exist”. Cage tried it on himself when, in the fifties, he went into an anechoic chamber (Wikipedia says that it is a “non-echoing or echo-free” room and I have no reason to doubt that) and he was rather disappointed when he realised that he could hear two tones: one high and one low. Someone explained to him that it was his nervous system and the blood in his veins that were making all that noise. Therefore there is no solution: silence does not exist. So what does Cage’s famous piece “4:33” tell us about ourselves? Four minutes and thirty-three seconds of apparent silence. The orchestra is there, the piano lid is up and everything is in place but nothing happens. Well, apparently, at least.
