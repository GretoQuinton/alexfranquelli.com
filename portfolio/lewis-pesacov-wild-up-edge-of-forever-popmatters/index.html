Lewis Pesacov & wild Up - Edge of Forever

Archive source

Full text recovered from Alex Franquelli’s author archive.

When a dear friend (nice shoes, non smoker, bad music taste) returned from seeing the Red Hot Chilli Peppers in Europe a few years ago, she thought it would be a good idea to give yours truly a call in order to file a formal complaint to the music industry. Which, with me being a mere freelance journalist, sounded a bit like when your mum calls you to protest because “Google has disappeared” from her computer. What were those freaks doing on stage? Why did the might RHCP invited them to open their gig? Good karma, I replied, to which I got no reaction. “But what about us”, poor fans “who have spent a fortune” to see Anthony Kiedis and the most talked about socks in the rock music industry, she cried? So I rushed to hear the band that dared standing between the icons of pop) formerly gods of funk) and their loyal albeit musically ill equipped fandom. Suddenly, everything became clear. Lewis Pesacov is one half of Fool’s Gold, a collective whose good inspiration is directly proportional to the magnitude of its following.

Their mixture of world rhythms and krautrock may not appeal to the masses, but it has definitely put this act on the map. So what happened when Pesacov met one of the freshest “experimental music companies” in Los Angeles? I am not sure, but the result is undoubtedly interesting. With a libretto by Elizabeth Cline and five scenes depicting the story of an a ancient astronomer who is so far from what he loves the most, trapped in the intricacies of time and space. Desidus is one of the most fascinating words whose evolution has reached us almost identical: desire. The prefix “de”, a negation, and “sidus”, or “star” in English, ideally sum up the philosophical content of Cline’s libretto. The word “desire”, or “starless” describes a world in which the object of our fascination remains unreachable, unattainable, and therefore painfully present in our lives. This immanence is in dire conflict with the very nature of this opera, which, has been performed the first and last time in Los Angeles, on the very day when, according to an alternative take on the Mayan calendar, the world was supposed to end. Once: because it would be marvellous to cease being while listening to this single tenor fluctuate through musical throes of primitivism and avant-garde. Once: because this is the only way in which its balance and purity are preserved.

Musically, The Edge of Forever, is a chamber opera comprising of – beside tenor Ashley Faatoalia – four female voices and an ensemble that sees two conch shell trumpets, woodwinds, strings, eight sine tone oscillators and a small array of traditional percussions. Less musically dense that like Ligeti’s Mysteries of the Macabre, equally dramatic but more direct, minimalist than John Adams’ The Rape of Lucretia, Edge of Forever is a work that develops in vertical fashion, with emotional ups and downs, crescendos and climatic, aimed at a dimension in which astronomy and metaphysics are the very warp and woof of the music proposed. Echoes of Japanese gagaku in “Ripples, Murmurs, Ripples, Sighs, Ripples, Hums" a vague reference to Stockhausen’s Drei Lieder, Pesacov’s music is a rare and delicate voice in today’s contemporary classical music, whose frailty (its unique performance) is the medium through which it solidifies into history. No evolution, no past, no present: just future. Then another cycle will end with another opera.
