Alx and Hanging the Star

Live at Bush Hall

London, 10 February 2012

A hundred and ninety-six seconds. From silence to silence; from the delicate violence of the streets of Shepherd’s Bush to the violent melancholy of a melody that echoes in everybody’s mind until the lights go off and the music stops again.

Kristy Clark introduces herself as an “Aussie”. Simple, like her music, which appears to be quintessential to her being and her singing. Quintissential in such a way that figuring out what it will sound like on an album, far from her presence, away from her timidly careful smiles seems as outrageous as talking during “Snowflakes”; a track that not even technical glitches and annoying feedback can spoil.

Kristy’s rendition of “Over the Rainbow” is the perfect synthesis of depth and emotion, which she balances with a very personal approach to folk that is somehow reminiscent of an early Joan Baez but with a lightness and naturalness which are proper of a musical virginity untouched by the routine of devotion to standardised songwriting.

“Find a Way” ends a gig that has shown a young singer with a complex personality that is yet to be discovered, but whose voice and inspiration offer a rare and compelling sensitivity.

A few moments pass and the Bush Hall remerges in all its anachronistic splendour adorned by red velvet drapes, plasterwork, mirrors and a decadent baroque feel which counters the decadent dryness of the streets and stall outside. Alx and Hanging the Star take the stage and the Hall fills with people at the back while an apparent tension contributes to the building up of expectations. For those who witnessed Alx’s performance with Bruce Soord of Pineapple Thief fame at the Union Chapel last year, the opening “Remember the Idiot”, although only recently published on the “Concussion” EP and therefore relatively unknown, brings back the memory of a performance which proved that Alx could be much more than a backing vocalist. In fact she is.

A listener’s mind plays funny tricks sometimes and this is probably one of the reasons why the first name that comes to mind when listening to the opening track is Beth Orton. The two singers are apparently dissimilar in style, but writing a song with an open mind requires a talent in itself and the two ladies seem to have it aplenty.

One may be inclined to think that when an artist is supported by a band (or a “collective”, to use Alx’s words) which less than an hour earlier was gracing that same venue with good taste and jazz standards, then momentum will drive a great performance anyhow.

That assumption is entirely justified, but wrong in this case, since Ms. Leeks seems to become the focus of everybody’s attention just the same way it happened in the gothic solemn Union Chapel.

“Slow” and the beautifully crafted “Catalyst”, a track which heeds it’s own advice(“Take your time / There is no use in speeding”) demonstrates what we think is the finest of this powerful yet delicate voice. But the best is yet to come and when the collective (only recently joined by Kristy Clark on backing vocals) leaves the stage and Alx sits alone at the piano, it looks as if the lights are dimming but they are not. “Sentimental” by Porcupine Tree is sang in a way that could be defined as “blues” in the least musical kind of ways. There is poetry and a hint of pain driving her fingers, while her voice adopts an even deeper tone toying with the dynamics of a song which unfolds in all its glorious despair. In In the audience, Steve Wilson nods.

“Northern Lad” by Tori Amos is simply a great song performed by an extremely talented singer, but the peak touched by the previous number somehow spoils what remains a poignantly beautiful track.

There is time for a couple more songs with the collective on stage once again. The elegant drumming of Jimmy Norden seems to lead the rest of the collective towards a musical apex, “Cigarette Song”, which is undoubtedly one of the highlights of the evening.

Something more than three minutes to confirm that Alx Leeks can definitely write and perform at the highest level. In more than three minutes and we are back to the cold. A hundred and ninety-six seconds. It’s all that it takes.
