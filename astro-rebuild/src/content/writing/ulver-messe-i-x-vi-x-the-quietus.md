<p>Ulver<br />Messe I.X–VI.X</p>

<p>(Alex Franquelli)</p>

<p>There is an overarching decadent feel that permeates every single note on Messe I.X–VI.X. And it is an unavoidable and almost imperceptible aura the listener if left dealing with, once the last note has ceased reverberating.</p>

<p>Ulver has been haunted by the likes of Górecki, Mahler and Holst for quite some time, true, but the real, subtle distinctness between the Norwegians and the contemporary purveyors of mainstream contemporary music  (Yiruma, Ludovico Einaudi, Michael Rosenzweig, to name but a few) lies in the different degrees of adaptability and contemporariness, which one can easily (and, ultimately, subjectively) assign to each and every composer. In other words: if Giovanni Allevi’s Evolution (surely, a title that is a clever act of Italian humour at its best) is destined to remain modern, alas, for the next 20 years, Messe I.X–VI.X is so present-day and compelling, that its newness is indissolubly connected to today’s taste and principles. Blame the band’s peculiar background, or their being new (although not entirely) to the language spoken on the album, or even the fact that they managed to capture a glimpse of their artistic restlessness - of their electronic soul – with a classical lens. But this album is a challenging work and an effort of rare beauty, that could definitely not have been written in any other period from any other artistic perspective.</p>

<p>The project in question saw the light when Norway’s Tromsø Kulturhus commissioned new music to Ulver, in participation with the Arctic Opera and Philharmonic Orchestra. The band was aided by composer Martin Romberg, whose penchant for folk, and fantasy in particular, can be clearly heard on the orchestration in various parts of the album, like the beautiful crescendo in “Glamour Box (Ostinati)” or the faintly audible interaction with the band’s electronics in ‘Noche Oscura del Alma’. The two musical entities performed the pieces in September 2012 and the final result is the consequence of many a tampering and alteration consequently carried out by Ulver. Therefore not a live album in the literal sense, Messe I.X–VI.X posed a challenge to the band (Kristoffer Rygg, Ole Aleksander Halstensgård, Jørn H. Sværen and Tore Ylwizaker), which had to, once again, somehow tame its inspiration and adapt its groove to the dynamics of a 21-piece ensemble.</p>

<p>The final result is a record where one finds it almost impossible to separate the band from the orchestra; the dialectical interaction does not make the music: it is the music. Take the opener ‘As Syrians Pour in, Lebanon Grapples with Ghosts of a Bloody Past’, for instance, where the strings are, at first, almost a muffled interference rumbling in the background before they take centre stage for a finale vaguely recalling Arvo Pärt’s ‘Fratres’ and its religious ambience. And it is religion, which is probably the main protagonist of the album. Ulver’s current stance on morality and spirituality is rather ambiguous, almost cerebral, if not subliminal. Pärt, Olivier Messiaen, but even John Taverner’s aesthetic approach, rather than his choral harmonies, and György Ligeti’s a-religious ‘Requiem’: all of them are re-elaborated and filtered through 20 years of music and experimentalism. </p>

<p>In the same fashion as Ligeti’s masterpiece, Ulver’s ‘Messe’ can be considered a Christian mass stripped of its liturgy, or an earthly prayer written by a man for a mortal audience. This is reflected in the lyrics to ‘Mother of Mercy’, where the entity on the receiving end appears in all her sensuality and terrestrial compassion (Oh Mother / Mother Of Mercy / Cradle Of All / Devotion And Desire / I Turn To You / From The Valley Of Tears / Carry Me As A Child / As A Son Of Man / Bathed In Light / And Precious Blood / Filling The Cup / Of The Skull Of Adam […]).</p>

<p>The essence of a gem like Shadows of the Sun certainly echoes in the background, but at the same time its presence does not interfere with the musical flux. Rygg’s vocals, for example, are an auxiliary medium confined to the last two tracks, where the ceremony reaches its climax and the final, mundane communion is reaffirmed by a group of old Italian women filling the air with their pleads to the Holy Virgin. The circle closes, the decadence can rise.</p>
