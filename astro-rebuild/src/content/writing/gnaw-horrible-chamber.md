<p>GNAW<br />HORRIBLE CHAMBER<br />(Alex Franquelli)</p>

<p>There is metal and metal: I agree. The impossibility of determining the exact boundaries of a genre has haunted many a writer (especially those with lots of time on their hands) with conflicting results.</p>

<p>But, still, there is metal and metal, and the huge difference, in 2013, is given by its attitude. Take Gnaw, for instance: does a track like the malignant, sludgy ‘For Embers’ sound like a tune which could suddenly surprise, or is it clear where it will end fifty-five seconds in? The abrupt, jazzy saxophone insertions on Kayo Dot’s ‘The Sow Submits’ are unexpected, original and startling; the obsessive, percussive pace on Gnaw’s ‘Vulture’ is predictable, derivative and definitely expected, as it invariably channels the song back into the metal limbo without making too much of an effort.</p>

<p>But since the quality of a record is not to be judged on originality alone, this album is simply boring. The fact that Alan Dubin (Khanate and O.L.D.) and Carter Thornton (Slaughter and Enos), together with Brian Beatrice, Eric Neuser and Jun Mizumachi (Ike Yard) are involved, makes them all the more guilty. Belluine shrieks? Check. Sludge a la Eyehategod? Check. Uncompromised heaviness throughout the album? Are you kidding me? Check! What’s missing? The mandatory suffocating feel, the claustrophobic (reviewers love this adjective!) pace and the buzzing guitars? Check, check, and definitely check.</p>

<p>Having panned out the relevant elements that constitute the aesthetic core of Horrible Chamber, one is able to come up with an opinion which, it should be pointed out, still misses the practical aspect of it all: a good old listen. What you expect from an album is a great outcome, and, nobody can even consider doubting this: Horrible Chamber does what it says on the tin. It is a tense, pounding act of musical corrosion that is constantly on the verge of degenerating into pure noise. The drones that embellish ‘Widowkeeper’, for instance, are instrumental to depicting the depravity of a doom that permeates the second half of the track, but if one considers the bigger picture, they do their job everywhere else on the album, too. They are, in fact, carefully administered in a way that was completely oblivious to Dubin’s previous creature, Khanate. Gnaw’s sound is mediated by the need to forge their crust and hardcore influences in order to obtain an alloy which, once the circle closes, is, wait for it, ultimately ‘metal’.</p>

<p>It is nihilistic and, yes, claustrophobic, but it’s not completely hopeless. The impression is that the components may and should be re-shaken and the long-term balance (un)carefully altered. Only looking at what there is out of the box can save a whole movement from dying a slow (even in terms of BPM) and painful expiration. This is a romantic end, but an end nonetheless.</p>
