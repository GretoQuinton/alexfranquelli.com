Overcoming the challenges of running multi--currency, multi--language and
multi--tax merchandising systems


Omni--channel is undoubtedly the new buzzword. This new, synergetic approach
is important for retail and is rightly being analysed and debated in publications
and conferences. But what are the challenges a business encounters when it opts
to follow that route? What is clear is that Omni--channel is all about providing the
best possible support to the ultimate beneficiary, the client, regardless of the
channel they decide to use to purchase, and this implies a complete redesign of
the multi--currency, multi--language and multi--tax platforms. But what are the
main challenges behind making this shift?


Multi--channel: already a thing of the past?
The multi--channel approach has clearly taught us that consumers are able to
make their own choices, thus privileging one route at the expense of another.
The result has been a customer--centric perspective, whose significance and
potential have somehow not been fully understood by most companies.

The messages carried by the cross--platform experience were not metabolised or
worse, understood by those enterprises, and the fragmentation that ensued
affected not only the very essence of the customers experience, but went as far as
influencing the buyers’ perception of the brand’s consistency. On the other hand,
what failed to materialise was an adequate processing of the inflow of data,
preventing businesses from settling on the right strategy.


Omni--channel: “a fantastic opportunity”
The omni--channel approach allows the brand to better track the customer across
a number of channels while, at the same time, providing an experience that is not
affected by the numerous routes the buyer can potentially take. The idea is to
create a customary experience and an increase in interaction with the brand
itself, rather than the single channel. Moreover, the purchasing data derived from
multiple channels needs to be unified in order to create a single customer profile,
thus generating a specific database allowing the creation of an accurate
characterisation of the individual clients. According to Brian Rosen, of Evolution
Wines & Spirits, “Omni--channel retailing is a fantastic opportunity: the more you
understand its dynamics, the more Omni--channel will create opportunities for
you and your business, and the more you get to grips with all the different
tentacles, the more in demand you’ll be”.


The consequent challenge behind this transformation lies in the integration of all
resources: an intricate network of information crossing not only platform, but
also world regions, thus involving an indefinite number of currencies, languages
and tax systems. For Rosen, “customer data is great, but sometimes you may risk
dealing with an information overload that blurs the line between relevant and
irrelevant data. You break the potential silos by going from the most money--
consuming one to the least important one. At the end of the day, you can take
into consideration a high volume of information, but nothing matters more than
the supply and demand balance”.

According to Fabrice Etienne, International Marketing Director of Rich
Relevance, “The number one priority is definitely about being Omni--channel and
reaching across all the different devices. Businesses recognise that their
customers are now everywhere at any time, and they really need to address that.
They are asking ‘How do I get more personalised? How do I get closer to my
customers?’ and ‘How do I reach them with my same brand message, but then
still remain relevant in terms of what they are looking for?’”


The multi--currency, multi--language and multi--tax merchandising systems:
more than just buzzwords?
But what happens once these processes are brought into an even larger arena? In
a world where an ever--increasing connectivity seems to be the only stable trend,
the shift from localisation to globalisation has ceased being a peculiarity of large
enterprises, and even local businesses have to find automated solutions for
managing their payments, irrespective of the sales channel they use. The basic
assumption is that customers are more comfortable paying in their home
currency. For this reason, multi--currency pricing is an extremely valuable sales
tool that helps merchants expand internationally and build their business while
differentiating themselves from their competitors.

However, in these kinds of transactions, the potential risks cannot be overstated.
“On a very high level, everybody wants to grow and scale their business”, says
Lance Dannic, Director of Sales, Kount, a leading innovator of ecommerce
solutions designed to Boost Sales and Beat Fraud for businesses working in card--
not--present environments. “We hear about those challenges”, he continues,
“businesses want to expand and break into new international markets; they
leverage the different channels and optimize them through social media and now
through mobile payments, which is an emerging payment type and technology.
And there is an inherent risk in all those different channels of growth. From our
experience working with our clients, a lot of the growth can get stunted at any
one point as it pertains to fraud. We’ve had many merchants that try to break
into new markets and expand internationally and because of the instances of
fraud, they have completely shut off those markets. The price of getting this
wrong can be huge – it can mean everything. If you are not keeping up with the
pace of technology, you will be outrun by the competition”.

The very essence of any successful international sales strategy lies in the ability
to present the products in the customer's language and, more importantly,


pricing. “It is all about the product you are trying to sell”, says Rosen. “That will
determine your strategy and will command different prices. Currency is
something you cannot fully control, but you must at least know the market
you’re going into”. The main challenge facing those enterprises willing to expand
abroad is, in fact, the ability to enable their businesses to easily and adequately
customise their offerings in order to attract new international buyers. “Pre--
market research is key. Get to hear what your target needs”, continues Rosen,
“and try to have a good appreciation of what the new market can bear. In a way,
understanding the environment you’re entering means understanding your own
product and vice versa”.

Companies can set prices in the currencies of the markets they sell in, while
continuing to receive payments in their own currency. In order to achieve that,
an accurate tracking of currency conversions is definitely crucial. A reliable
multi--currency system, for instance, assists and simplifies the tracking of
revenue and expenses, converted from foreign to domestic currency, by making
sure that the exact exchange fluctuations are used. Such a complex system has to
inevitably rely on a dependable set of procedures, on the interaction between
technology and human resources, and the administration of multiple payment
gateways, prices and shipping options requiring constant monitoring. For this
reason, multi--currency is a viable option only if the website generates enough
international volume. Moreover, dealing with various currencies implies the
acceptance and integration of the localised laws, rules and bodies.

Because of this, the multi--tax question is yet another major challenge facing a
business entering the global arena, as e--commerce and, more generally,
international retail, often have to come to terms with a multijurisdictional
framework where different taxes and rates apply to different volumes of sales.
Moreover, being aware of the rules and regulations governing cross--border trade
is vitally important, as the institutional processes keep on evolving, mainly as a
consequence of the asymmetries and different developments of international
trade and e--commerce themselves. Technology and a solid management system
can facilitate not only the administration of transactions involving multiple
currencies but they can, at the same time, keep the company up to speed with tax
and cross--border tax information. Thus drastically reducing the costs and the
risks associated with the different duty rates and regulations.

In addition to this, the production of global content requires the utilisation of a
language – both metaphorically and literally– that is both colloquial and
accurate, in order to earn the market’s trust and confidence.

The final results may not appear visible at a first glance, but going the extra mile
to try and design integrated technology systems and efficient workflows to back
a series of mobile, physical and online channels can be a rewarding investment.
It requires an integration involving the sales, marketing and IT departments, all
listening to different voices, all focused on one, definite goal.
